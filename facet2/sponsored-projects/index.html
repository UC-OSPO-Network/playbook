Sponsored awards

What are sponsored awards?¶

At UC, research awards are classified as either gifts or sponsored awards (read the policy here). “Sponsored awards” is an umbrella term that covers a variety of agreements including grants, subawards, sponsored research agreements, some fellowships, consulting agreements, and more. In sponsored awards, a sponsor awards funds to the university, and the university provides some sort of benefit to the funder (usually research outputs).

Whether a project is a sponsored award, and if so which kind, determines which university office will handle its administration. (Procedures vary by campus, but usually the SPO is your go-to office for sponsored awards.) How an award is classified by UC has implications for how the money can be used, the level of flexibility regarding the expected outcomes, and the level of reporting that is required.

Grants¶

With the exception of mature commercial OSS (COSS) businesses, grants are usually a major portion of a healthy open source project’s portfolio. Nearly all of the projects we interviewed received grant funding at one time or another. Most often, the grant funding came early on, helping the project get off the ground. On the other hand, some projects, like AAQ21 and AAQ21, received their first grant years after their inception.

The most well-known type of grant is probably the federal grant. Federal agencies such as the NSF, NIH, DOE, and DOD are major funders of academic research, with just under half of UC researchers’ grant funds coming from the federal government.

Several relationships are worth cultivating when it comes to federal grants. First, get to know your campus’ central SRI office. Note the relevant support person for your field, and explore the SRI’s resources, including their newsletter, website, events, tools, and training opportunities. Get to know your department’s grants liaison, as well. They can help you identify additional opportunities and craft a solid proposal. Engaging with these support staff can give you a competitive edge, and is crucial for limited submissions, i.e. grants with a cap on the number of proposals per institution. Finally, some of our interviewees recommended maintaining a good relationship with the program manager from the relevant directorate or department within a federal agency of interest.

AAQ25

Some faculty feel they should focus on major federal grants, in part because they offer large sums of money, and in part because they may be viewed as prestigious. However, a diverse portfolio is a robust one. Securing grants from a variety of funders makes your portfolio more resilient to disaster, demonstrates your “fundability”, helps sharpen your grant writing skills, and allows you to pursue diverse projects.

Your campus may also offer internal grants. These grants tend to be for smaller amounts and/or time frames. The Office of Research, provost, chancellor, and independent research units may offer seed grants, intended to help a new research initiative acquire the preliminary data needed to be competitive for larger grants. Other university departments may offer grants that go toward improving the campus culture, bolstering students in need, or reducing the campus’ environmental footprint. Travel grants for conferences are also not uncommon. At most UC campuses, one department maintains a list of internal grant opportunities, such as that maintained by UCLA’s Research Enhancement Office, or UC Irvine’s internal Fundopp platform.

Grant writing tip for open source projects

It is well known that grant reviewers often look for novelty, which can make it difficult to fund software maintenance. To fund your software maintenance efforts, add some components that are novel. For example, you might:

  • find a collaborator who needs a certain software feature in order to answer a novel research question

  • propose upgrades that will enable investigation of new questions

  • propose generalizing the software so it will be useful to more people in more fields, again, opening up new questions

Think about ways in which the software updates will help generate new insights. Also, if your proposed software upgrades will open the door to new relationships, and new potential funders, contributors, or customers, saying so may strengthen your proposal.

Foundation and society grants can be a great way to fund software development and maintenance. Some foundations specifically fund open source software, such as the Mozilla Foundation, the Python Foundation, the Open Technology Fund, the Chan Zuckerberg Institute, and the Sloan Foundation. There may also be relevant foundations specific to your academic discipline, such as the Patrick J. McGovern Foundation, the Doris Duke Foundation, the American Cancer Society, or the Entertainment Software Foundation. Some foundations may be interested only in very particular aspects of your research program (often translational aspects), while others may want to invest in you (or your trainee) as a person, more than a particular project. It depends on their goals and your relationship. When looking for foundation and society grants, prioritize societies you want to build a lasting relationship with. If their networking events, webinars, and volunteer opportunities appeal to you, that bodes well for long-term success.

Industry grants can help you develop the translational aspects of your research program, and can be a buffer against changes in political climate and federal funding. At least anecdotally, many or most industry-academia collaborations arise out of a particular need, problem, or interest, rather than from a broad request for proposals (RfP). Nevertheless, some technology companies do offer RfP-style grants and fellowships for academics working in open source. These grants may be focused on translational solutions relevant to the company’s products, or social or charitable issues like climate change or STEM education. Industry grants can lead to rewarding projects that have an impact on society. They can also attract student researchers who are interested in an industry career. Your campus may have a CRO or OD that can inform you about opportunities to connect with industry.

There are robust resources at UC to help you acquire grants. SRI and departmental grant liaisons assist faculty and other academic senate members with identifying opportunities and writing proposals. They may also have licenses to tools such as Pivot or Spin that can help you identify funding opportunities, and they can train you in how to use those tools. For assistance with student or postdoctoral fellowships, look to the Graduate Division. They may have workshops, peer or staff advisors, and other resources.

Nearly all of the projects we interviewed have received grant funding at one time or another. On the other hand, many also encountered the well-known limitations of grant funding: complex submission and post-award administration processes, limited duration, and (often) preference for novelty over infrastructural “glue work” and software maintenance (Feng, 2023, Research Software Alliance, 2022).

AAQ24

While some projects, such as AAQ26 or AAQ26 may enjoy decades of federal grant funding, these cases are the exception. In general, grant funding–be it public or private–is typically about spending money to make money. Most grants are meant to seed a project that will become increasingly self-sustaining as it grows. Use your grant funding to get preliminary data or build prototypes, rather than regarding grants as a permanent funding source in themselves.

Contracts¶

In addition to grants, there are many other types of formal agreements that can be formed between university researchers and sponsors (i.e. governments, companies, and/or foundations). Whereas grant-funded projects are usually proposed by the research team, contract work is often solicited by the sponsor (Source: UCSF). Research contracts may in some ways be harder to come by than grants, because they often lack an open or transparent application process. On the other hand, they often bypass the heated competition of an open call, and can be negotiated to suit all parties.

A contract is an agreement that stipulates certain deliverables, obligations, and rights for all parties. A common use of sponsored research contracts is when the principal purpose of the agreement is to provide products or services directly to the sponsor. Payment is contingent upon timely, satisfactory delivery of the product or service. The most common type of contract on UC campuses is the cost-reimbursement contract, in which the sponsor pays for the costs incurred in the conduct of the work up to an agreed-upon amount (as opposed to a fixed-price agreement) (Sources: UCB, UCLA).

Industry Sponsored Research Agreements (SRAs) are a common type of contract in academic-industry collaborations (Source: UCSB). These agreements can have strict deliverables or be fairly exploratory. Your campus SPO (usually–campuses vary) will be key in drawing up a contract that works for everyone, and they can be your ally when negotiating on terms such as compensation, reporting, or staffing. When working with a sponsor, always make sure there’s something good in it for you.

Contract negotiations often entail decisions about intellectual property rights. At this stage it is crucial to clarify with your sponsor which outputs will be open source. While some organizations will be excited about open source, for others, this stipulation will be a deal-breaker.

AAQ22

If you are interested in collaborating with a potential sponsor, take advantage of UC’s many support staff who can help. Your departmental SPO can answer any questions about contracts. Your campus may also have a CRO that can help you identify potential sponsors. Your campus’ Conflict of Interest Coordinator can also help you understand when that relationship might constitute a conflict of interest. Make sure the work aligns with your research goals, and watch out for future rewards being conditioned–even subtly or implicitly–on certain results (Kafaee et al. 2021). If the agreement involves an international third party, special policies may apply, which your SPO and ORI can assist with.

Industry Consortia¶

Your interactions with industry need not be one-to-one. Your campus may host or belong to several industry consortia. These are collaborative partnerships where multiple companies pay a membership fee to fund pre-competitive academic research in a shared field of strategic interest. Consortium dues can fund personnel that may be hard to fund with grants, such as Research Software Engineers (RSEs), technical writers, or community managers. Consortium dues also may qualify for reduced Facilities & Administrative (F&A) overhead rates compared to standard sponsored research contracts, depending on campus policy.

AAQ16

If you are interested in joining a particular industry consortium, ensure that it is friendly to open source. Check whether existing consortium agreements explicitly mandate or allow that all core deliverables be released under open-source licenses. It’s also good to clarify how contributor license agreements (CLAs) work when corporate engineers contribute code to the research code base. You should also determine the consortium’s stance on patents.

AAQ23

For examples of UC industry consortia, explore UC Davis’ Industry Consortia, UC Berkeley’s Industry Alliances, or the cross-campus UC Drug Discovery Consortium. There may also be industry-sponsored shared facilities on your campus, such at the UC Santa Barbara SSLEEC. Check with your campus TTO and/or CRO to see if an existing consortium aligns with your software. If not, and you have a network of 3–5 companies using your tool, talk to them about launching one!
