Community: the secret sauce of open source

For all its challenges, open source has one huge advantage over its proprietary competitors: community. Unlike proprietary software, open source software has a strategic reserve of community labor that gives it a competitive edge. While so-called “casual contributors” are often invisible to the average user, they make up about half of open source contributors, and about one-third of “casual contributions” are bug fixes (Pinto, Steinmacher, and Aurélio Gerosa 2016).

In addition to coding labor, a community provides value in other ways. Community members can advocate for the product at their institution, at their company, or among their colleagues, promoting adoption and new partnerships. They can also make your product appealing to a more diverse user base by giving feedback on the user experience, suggesting new features, or writing documentation translations. In open source, trust, reputation, and diversity are currencies as real as money (Wermke et al. 2022, Alarcon et al. 2020, De Filippi and Loveluck, 2016, Rock, Grant, and Grey 2016), and these are built through effective community management.

For most successful open source projects, attention to the community is not optional. Of the CHAOSS project’s 17 metrics models for measuring the health of an open source software project, 11 relate to some aspect of group cooperation, while only 6 cover other topics such as finances, security, and business strategy. In other words, it is likely the vibrancy of your community—not your funding portfolio or your product quality—that people will look to when assessing whether your project is healthy and trustworthy.

Three Approaches to OSS Communities¶

Before we dig in, here’s a quick way to tell how much of this facet is for you. Open source projects vary a lot in how much they set out to involve other people, and it helps to picture that as a set of rings/levels:

  • Level 1: In the innermost level, you have users but you’re not endeavoring on their behalf as such. You’ve made your software available, and people can use it or fork it, but you’re not fielding requests or taking contributions. That’s a completely legitimate way to run an open source project! If that’s where you are and where you’re happy to stay, you’re in good shape, and you can skip most of this facet. It’ll be here if your project ever grows into wanting a community around it.

  • Level 2: The next level is where you start engaging with your users. You take requests, you help people when they get stuck, but you’re not accepting outside contributions to the code.

  • Level 3: The outermost level is full co-development: you take requests and you welcome contributions from beyond your core team.

The distinctions between these rings or levels are not always clear (nor, for that matter, is the distinction between user and contributor always clear). Nevertheless, there can be no mistaking that some open source projects are more community-oriented than others, and these three levels are a useful starting point for talking about that variation.

Our interviewees told us again and again about the importance of nurturing community: answering questions, hosting workshops, and investing in relationships. In this facet, we share their tried-and-true strategies for success. We tailor this advice to the three levels shown above, so you can read the advice that fits where your project actually is.

Related resources¶

Want to learn more? Check out these general resources for open source community.

  • The Turing Way

    • Community is a major theme embedded in the entire Turing Way, a handbook for reproducible research. Their community documents and processes are also public and very readable, meaning you can also learn from their example.

  • CSCCE: Center for Scientific Collaboration and Community Engagement

    • CSCCE is a project dedicated to training and support for people managing scientific communities. If you are a full-time or part-time community manager, check out their wealth of resources and their certification programs.

  • The Open Source Way

    • A collection of best practices for initiating, nurturing, growing, and maintaining open source communities.
