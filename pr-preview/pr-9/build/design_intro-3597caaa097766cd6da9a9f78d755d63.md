We first envisioned this chapter based on a simple hypothesis: open source design is about building a codebase others can push code to. An open source codebase should be readable, modular, secure, and interoperable with popular frameworks. All of these things are true, but again and again, we heard from our interviewees that open source design is about so much more than code.

Relatively few projects we discussed in our interviews receive regular community contributions to the core codebase. In almost all cases, the core codebase was maintained primarily by one to several people, with community-contributed code being fairly unusual. Nevertheless, all projects depended heavily on community contributions of various kinds.

In this section, we’ll discuss best practices for building a readable codebase. Then, we’ll discuss a key topic that emerged from our interviews: the different types of community contribution. We present five “contribution models”, or the five most common ways (based on our interviews) that the general public contributes their time and energy to improving open source projects.

Like open source business models (see the [Entrepreneurship section](../facet2/entrepreneurship.md)), we believe that these contribution models provide a framework for articulating an aspect of your project’s sustainability plan. Just like business models can help you articulate how you will make money, these contribution models can help you articulate how you will attract new contributors.

From our first interview, we had difficulty separating the topics of design and community. Eventually, we had to admit that we were trying to separate the inseparable. Open source design is about clean code, yes, but it’s also about inviting community. Part of open source design is choosing where to place the on-ramp for users to become contributors. Placing that on-ramp in front of your codebase is just one option. Here, we’ll discuss that option plus some others.
